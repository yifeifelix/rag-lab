# Lesson 4 · Searching by keywords ("BM25")

> **In one sentence:** BM25 scores a card by how many of your question's words it contains -
> giving extra points for **rare** words and not over-rewarding a word that's repeated many times.

**Time:** about 45 minutes · **You will:** meet a second librarian who searches by exact words,
build its scoring rule from two simple ideas, and see when it beats the meaning-based librarian.

---

## Why we need a second librarian

In Lesson 2 you probably saw the meaning-based librarian stumble on things like
`shutil.copytree` (an exact function name). It knows `copytree` is "something about copying", so it
may bring back a card about `copy` or `copy2` instead. Close in meaning - but **wrong**.

Think of the index at the back of a printed book. If you look up **"copytree"**, it lists exactly
the pages containing that word. It doesn't understand anything, but it never confuses
`copytree` with `copy`. That's keyword search.

**BM25** is the most widely used keyword-search scoring method. It's from the 1990s, and it's
still the default in big search engines like Elasticsearch. The name just means "Best Matching,
attempt number 25" - the researchers tried many versions!

| | Meaning librarian (Lessons 2-3) | Keyword librarian (this lesson) |
|---|---|---|
| Good at | different words, same meaning (*"pause"* → *"delay"*) | exact names, codes, rare words (`copytree`, `TimeoutExpired`) |
| Bad at | exact names and codes | synonyms - *"pause"* won't find *"delay"* |

They are good at opposite things. That's why, in Lesson 5, we'll combine them.

## Step 1 · Split text into words ("tokenizing")

Before counting words, we need to decide what a "word" is. Our tokenizer:

- makes everything lowercase (so *Queue* and *queue* match),
- drops very common words like *how*, *does*, *the* (they appear everywhere and tell us nothing),
- keeps dotted names like `json.dumps` together **and** also splits them, so both
  "json.dumps" and just "dumps" can match.

```{code-cell} ipython3
from raglab.retrieval import tokenize
print(tokenize("How does json.dumps handle NaN values?"))
```

**What you should see:** `['json.dumps', 'json', 'dumps', 'handle', 'nan', 'values']` -
*how* and *does* are gone.

## Step 2 · Idea one: rare words are worth more ("IDF")

Imagine you're looking for a friend in a crowd, and someone tells you:

- *"they have two eyes"* → useless, everyone does.
- *"they have bright green hair"* → very useful, almost nobody does.

Words work the same way. If a word appears on **almost every card** (like *return* or *value*),
finding it tells us little. If it appears on **only one or two cards** (like *rmtree*), it's a
strong clue - like the green hair.

So each word gets a **rarity score**, called **IDF** (short for *Inverse Document Frequency* -
"inverse" because the *fewer* cards contain the word, the *higher* the score).

To calculate it we need two numbers:

- **N** = total number of cards in the library
- **df** = how many cards contain this word (*df* stands for "document frequency")

The formula:

$$\text{IDF} = \ln\left(1 + \frac{N - df + 0.5}{df + 0.5}\right)$$

Don't worry about the exact shape - here's what it does with **N = 100 cards**:

| Word | appears on (df) | IDF score | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| `rmtree` | 1 card | 4.21 | super rare → big clue |
| `directory` | 10 cards | 2.26 | fairly rare → good clue |
| `return` | 90 cards | 0.11 | everywhere → almost worthless |

(`ln` is the "natural logarithm" - in Python, `math.log`. All you need to know: it grows slowly,
so super-rare words don't get absurdly huge scores.)

## Step 3 · Idea two: repetition helps, but only up to a point

If a card mentions *queue* 5 times, it's probably more about queues than a card that mentions it
once. But is a card that says *queue* 50 times really 50 times better? No - after a few
mentions, it's clearly about queues, and extra repeats add little.

Think of restaurant reviews: a review that mentions *"pizza"* three times is probably about
pizza. One that says it thirty times isn't thirty times more about pizza - it's just repetitive.

BM25 makes repetition **level off**. The box below draws this. The dashed line is "just count
repeats" (keeps growing forever); the solid lines are BM25 (they flatten out). The setting
`k1` controls how quickly it flattens.

```{code-cell} ipython3
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

times_mentioned = np.arange(0, 21)
for k1 in (0.5, 1.5, 3.0):
    plt.plot(times_mentioned, times_mentioned * (k1 + 1) / (times_mentioned + k1), label=f"BM25 with k1={k1}")
plt.plot(times_mentioned, times_mentioned / 4, "--", alpha=.5, label="plain counting (scaled)")
plt.xlabel("how many times the word appears on the card")
plt.ylabel("points given")
plt.legend(); plt.show()
```

**What you should see:** the solid curves rise fast at first and then flatten. The 1st and 2nd
mention matter a lot; the 15th barely matters.

### And one more fairness rule: card length

A word found on a **short** card is a stronger sign than the same word found on a **very long**
card. (Finding "pizza" in a one-line review means a lot; finding it once in a 50-page food
guide means little.) BM25 compares each card's length with the average length and slightly
lowers the points for long cards. The setting `b` (usually 0.75) controls how much.

---

## Putting it together: the points for one word on one card

For **one word** of the question on **one card**:

$$\text{points} = \text{IDF} \times \frac{tf \times (k_1 + 1)}{tf + k_1 \times \left(1 - b + b \times \frac{\text{card length}}{\text{average length}}\right)}$$

where **tf** ("term frequency") is how many times the word appears on the card.

Read it like this: **rarity** (IDF) × **repetition that levels off** (the fraction), where the
bottom of the fraction also includes the **length fairness rule**.

A card's total score for a question = the points for each question word, added together.

**Worked example** - word `rmtree` (IDF ≈ 4.2) appears once (tf = 1) on a card of average length
(card length = average length), with k1 = 1.5 and b = 0.75:

```text
length part:  1 - 0.75 + 0.75 × 1  = 1
fraction:     1 × 2.5 / (1 + 1.5 × 1) = 2.5 / 2.5 = 1
points:       4.2 × 1 = 4.2
```

## The two librarians, side by side

`BM25Retriever` puts your two formulas together (you can read it in `src/raglab/retrieval.py`).
The box below asks both librarians five questions and shows which document each one picks first:

- the first three questions are **exact names**,
- the last two are **everyday descriptions** using none of the documentation's words.

```{code-cell} ipython3
from raglab import load_documents, chunk_documents
from raglab.retrieval import BM25Retriever, VectorRetriever

docs = load_documents("python_docs")
cards = chunk_documents(docs, "recursive", max_chars=800)
keyword_librarian = BM25Retriever(cards)
meaning_librarian = VectorRetriever(cards)

for question in ["shutil.rmtree", "TimeoutExpired", "heappush",
                 "make my program wait a bit", "get rid of a folder and its contents"]:
    k = keyword_librarian.search(question, 1)
    m = meaning_librarian.search(question, 1)
    print(f"{question!r:40} keywords -> {k[0].doc_id if k else '(nothing found)':16} meaning -> {m[0].doc_id}")
```

**What you should see:** for the exact names, the keyword librarian finds the right document
(`py.shutil`, `py.subprocess`, `py.heapq`). For the everyday descriptions, the keyword librarian
often finds **nothing** or the wrong thing, while the meaning librarian does better.
Neither one wins every time.

## Common mix-ups

- **"BM25 understands the question."** It doesn't - it only counts words. *"cat"* and *"kitten"*
  are completely different to it.
- **"Old means bad."** BM25 is decades old and still very hard to beat for exact names, codes and
  product numbers. Many modern systems use it alongside embeddings.

## Recap

- BM25 = for each question word: **rarity** (IDF) × **repetition that levels off**, adjusted
  for card length. Add up over all words.
- It's fast, easy to explain ("these words matched"), and great for exact names.
- It fails on synonyms and rewording - the opposite weakness of the meaning librarian.
